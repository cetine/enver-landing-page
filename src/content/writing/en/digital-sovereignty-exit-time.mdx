Every conversation about digital sovereignty I have in Germany ends up on a map. Servers in Frankfurt? Then we are fine.

**The map answers the wrong question.** Where your data sits does not tell you who can order the provider to hand it over, or whether you could leave. I think sovereignty should be measured in a unit people can count: the time it takes to walk away.

I defended this argument by video link at the congress last week. The panel made it better; its objections are included below.

## Why server location is the wrong question

On 10 June 2025, Anton Carniaux, director of public and legal affairs at Microsoft France, testified under oath before a French Senate committee of inquiry. Asked whether he could guarantee that French citizens' data held by Microsoft would never be handed over on a US government order without France's explicit agreement, he answered: *"Non, je ne peux pas le garantir"*. No, I cannot guarantee it. It had never happened, he added.

Since the CLOUD Act of 2018, a US provider served with a US warrant or order must preserve or disclose data in its "possession, custody, or control" **"regardless of whether"** it is located inside or outside the United States (18 U.S.C. § 2713). The test is control, not geography. Location matters for privacy law, as I wrote about [sending redacted prompts to a model](/writing/gdpr-presidio-llm-privacy), but not for the question "what if the relationship breaks?"

And it can break. After US sanctions on the International Criminal Court, the Associated Press reported on 15 May 2025 that the court's chief prosecutor had lost access to his Microsoft email and moved to Proton Mail, a Swiss provider. Microsoft's statement of 28 May 2025 confirms that the sanctioned official was disconnected, but says it never stopped or suspended its services to the ICC as a whole. How it happened is disputed. That it happened is not.

## What is digital sovereignty, actually?

Here is the definition I propose: **sovereignty is the proven, contractually secured and practised ability to leave a provider, a platform or a jurisdiction within a defined time, without becoming unable to act.** Its unit is exit time.

Three things follow. **Dependency is allowed.** The problem is dependency with no way out. **The measure works in every direction**, towards Washington, Beijing and Paris alike. **It changes the negotiation.** Whoever can switch in twelve months is treated differently from whoever could not in twelve years.

Moving a workload into an EU region shifts it from bottom left to bottom right: the data sits in the Union, the contract is as hard to leave as before. The move that buys freedom is vertical: open formats, a written exit plan, a switch rehearsed at least once.

## Does the EU Data Act already solve cloud lock-in?

Partly. The Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) has applied since 12 September 2025, and its Chapter VI turns exit time into law. Under Art. 25, a cloud contract's notice period may not exceed two months, and the switch must then be completed within a transitional period of **at most 30 calendar days**. Where that is technically unfeasible, the provider may set up to seven months, and the customer may extend once. From **12 January 2027**, switching charges are banned for standard services (Art. 29(1)). Custom-built services are exempt (Art. 31), early-termination penalties remain possible, and an amendment to these rules is being negotiated.

So the law accepts the principle. But it guarantees that the provider lets your data go, not that you have somewhere to take it, people to run it, or a tested plan.

## What the Commission's new cloud law measures

On 3 June 2026 the Commission published its proposal for a Cloud and AI Development Act (COM(2026) 502). It is still being negotiated. Its core is **four Union assurance levels**. Level 1 is a self-assessment: provider, infrastructure and customer data in the EU. Levels 2 to 4 need third-party audits and tighten step by step, up to EU-citizen staff and no control from outside the EU. Public bodies would have to buy at least level 1, and levels 2 to 4 where a national risk assessment finds the activity protects public order, in sectors from energy to justice and defence (Arts. 29 and 30).

The annexes hold the surprise. At levels 2 and 3, a provider using security-relevant software from non-EU makers needs a "documented migration plan in the event that the vendor fails or a third country imposes restrictions" (Annex II). The indicative audit annex adds: **"Tests must be implemented and a switchover plan enabling migration"** (Annex III, point 9). The Commission already treats a rehearsed exit as a sovereignty test. It just aims it at the provider's suppliers, not at the customer.

A migration that a risk assessment requires "shall not exceed 12 months" (Art. 29(6)); otherwise the proposal leaves exit to the Data Act. **None of the criteria in Annex II asks how fast a public body could leave the provider it buys from.** That is the gap I would close.

## Where the argument gets uncomfortable

The sharpest objection on the panel was about money. Switching fees largely vanish in 2027, but the lost weeks, the retraining and the risk that something breaks in production are paid by nobody. Strict exit duties could also drown the small providers that might build alternatives.

That is right, so grade exit time by how critical a service is. For most services, a clause with open formats and an exit window costs almost nothing. Only the most critical deserve a tested reserve.

The second objection came from someone in uniform: in a real crisis, people switch because they have no choice. Maybe. But a crisis switch happens on the other side's terms. **Nobody negotiates a contract in an emergency.**

And some dependencies have no short exit at all. Europe will not replace the most advanced chips or a satellite network in weeks. There, the honest strategy is being indispensable to the other side.

## What I would put in every contract

Three questions belong in every public tender for cloud, software and AI. Anyone asks them before signing a phone contract.

1. **In which open format do we get our data back?**
2. **What is the exit time for this service?** Set per criticality level, with a penalty if missed.
3. **When did we last try?** Once a year for critical services.


## Bottom line

An EU region is a decision about geography. Sovereignty is a decision about time. The Data Act says how fast a provider must let you go. The missing piece is asking how fast we could leave.

Cloud and AI contracts signed now often run into the 2030s. Whether we can still choose then is decided today, in the small print.

The prize is named after Ariadne, whose part in defeating the Minotaur was not a weapon but a thread: the certainty of a way back. Europe will have to walk into many labyrinths, foreign clouds and foreign models among them. It may. Just never without a thread.
