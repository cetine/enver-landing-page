A CFO planning 2027 headcount this month has probably read that AI is now the No. 1 reason for US layoffs. The sentence is accurate. It is the wrong input for a plan.

**It is true only as a year-to-date sum.** Most of 2026's AI-cited cuts were announced between March and July, and 93.8% by the end of July. In August AI fell to fourth among stated reasons, in September to fifth. And the whole tally counts announcements, which are a sliver of the layoffs the US actually records.

So I rebuilt the monthly series from Challenger's nine 2026 reports, set it against the official count of actual US layoffs, and read the SEC filings of four large 2026 layoffs linked to AI.

## How many jobs lost to AI in 2026? What the count measures

Challenger's September report, released on 1 October 2026, gives the current number: "So far this year, AI has been cited in 120,136 job cut announcements, approximately 21% of all cuts, and it remains the leading reason year-to-date."

Three words carry the meaning. **Cited**: the reason the employer gave. **Announcements**: plans, not completed dismissals. **Year-to-date**: a running total that cannot fall.

The total is real. It is 2.2 times the 54,836 AI-cited cuts of all 2025, and, by my count from Challenger's cumulative figures, 62.6% of every AI-cited cut it has recorded since it began tracking the reason in 2023.

The first-half total, 101,743, was published on 1 July. It is three months stale.

After a small January and February, AI led every month from March to July, peaking in May at 38,579 cuts, 40% of everything announced that month. April and May together are half the year's AI total (50.0%).

Then it fell: 3,462 AI-cited cuts in August, 3,961 in September. The monthly average dropped from 16,102 (January to July) to 3,712 (August and September), **4.3 times lower**.

The reports do not name the companies behind the April-to-June counts; for May, Challenger says only that FinTech companies announced 5,731 cuts, "the bulk of which cited AI".

## What counts as an AI layoff

In July Challenger pointed to "the ambiguous nature of what constitutes AI-attributed cuts". Visa's 7% reduction, tied to an efficiency push in which AI would reshape work, was counted as AI. A separate bucket, "possibly AI", is used when AI "is alluded to but not directly tied to the cuts".

Challenger records plans announced "as a range or over multiple years" at "the announced figure", and files listings by "the location of the layoff or corporate headquarters", so non-US roles can enter the count.

Andy Challenger said in July: "Naming AI in a layoff announcement can win over investors while pushing current and prospective employees away." He added: "As regulations start to take shape, companies will be even more careful in their announcements, which would make tracking the impact of AI on jobs more opaque."

**That cuts both ways.** Part of the spring spike may have been messaging. Part of the autumn drop may be a retreat in wording rather than in cutting.

## Is AI still the No. 1 reason for US layoffs?

Year to date, yes, but only by 6,012 cuts over Market/Economic Conditions. At September's pace that reason gains about 4,828 a month on AI. As a projection, not a forecast, the lead narrows in October but survives.

Two low months are not yet a trend either. September was up on August, from 3,462 to 3,961 cuts and from 6.5% to 9.2% of the month.

## Announcements against actual layoffs

The BLS counts something different. Its Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) records "involuntary separations initiated by the employer" at US establishments, in the month they happen. From January to August 2026 that came to 13,491,000 layoffs and discharges, not seasonally adjusted (raw counts, before smoothing for seasonal patterns; the smoothed total, 13,814,000, tells the same story).

From January to August, the 116,175 AI-cited announcements equal **0.86% of actual layoffs**. All announcements together, 529,914, equal 3.9%.

This is not a matched share: one series counts plans, some global or multi-year, the other US separations as they occur. Read it as an order of magnitude.

Actual US layoffs from January to August were 1.9% lower than a year earlier. The information sector, which includes much of tech, is the exception: 392,000 against 290,000, up 35%, though July and August were below 2025. Industry figures are small and noisy; I read them as a pointer that the pain in tech is real, even if small in the total.

In Germany, the Bundesagentur für Arbeit reported 2,994,000 unemployed in September, a rate of 6.4%, and does not mention AI. In the year to July, manufacturing lost 169,400 jobs and information and communication 13,900: **12.2 times as many in manufacturing**, on projected July figures.

## What the filings say

8-K filings, which US-listed companies must file with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) after material events, put reasons next to charges. In the Block, Dow and HubSpot filings, the section on layoff costs (Item 2.05) does not name AI.

**Block** announced on 26 February a cut of more than 40% of staff, with charges of $450 million to $500 million. The shareholder letter is explicit: "Intelligence tools have changed what it means to build and run a company", taking Block "from over 10,000 people to just under 6,000". Challenger does not say where it counted Block: February's entire AI-cited total was 4,680.

**Dow**'s board approved on 26 January a reduction of about 4,500 roles globally, with severance of $600 million to $800 million. The press release reproduced in its 8-K ties a target of at least $2 billion in near-term operating earnings, in part, to "utilizing AI and automation".

**Intuit** announced a 17% reduction on 20 May, in AI's peak month, with charges of $300 million to $340 million. The CEO's email gives structural reasons only, starting with "too many organizational layers". AI appears as strategy, an "AI-native platform".

**HubSpot** filed on 6 October for about 7% of staff, nearly 660 people, at $65 million to $75 million, roughly $98,000 to $114,000 per role. The CEO memo says: "This is not driven by AI-related efficiencies." The same memo says the strategy has shifted to "delivering outcomes for them with AI." A denial is self-description, not evidence, and may be the safer message in a cut whose completion the 8-K makes "subject to local law and consultation requirements".

## What I would do when planning 2027 headcount

**Treat an "AI layoff" headline as a stated reason in an announcement.** It tells you what a company chose to say, and on what date, not how many people left or why.

**Read the filing, not the tally.** Headcount, charges and completion date are in the 8-K and its exhibits.

**Do not extrapolate the spring.** The monthly flow fell fourfold after July. A plan that assumes AI cuts are accelerating is projecting March to July forward.

**Plan the consultation, not the headline date.** HubSpot's own filing puts local law and consultation between decision and completion. That interval belongs in the timeline from the first draft.

## Bottom line

AI is the leading stated reason for US layoff announcements in 2026, and it got there in the five months from March to July. Since July it has been one reason among several, in a market where actual layoffs are slightly down. None of that supports a 2027 plan built on acceleration.

The No. 1 is a sum, and sums do not go down.
