Since TypeSafe launched Jev on 15 September, most banking conversations I have reach one question: should decision flows move to software that can "[act when confidence is high and escalate when it is not](https://typesafe.ai/)"?

That frames it as a model choice. It is not. **In a confidence-gated system, the durable asset is not the model but the threshold: a cut-off chosen on your own labels, valid for one pinned model version.** Swap the model and you recalibrate; you do not redeploy.

Earlier this year my team built that pattern for a German retail bank. This week we replayed 600 of its test messages through Jev, and why that took two days is the argument. *(The engagement is anonymised and composite; its figures, the replay's included, are illustrative. Everything else is sourced or was run on my laptop.)*

## What we built before Jev launched

The bank gets about 9,000 written dispute messages a month, four in five in German. Until June, people routed every one.

We added no model vendor; we extended the DPIA. An open-weight LLM in the bank's own EU cloud tenancy, whose DORA register entry now covers dispute intake, picks one of seven queues, gated on that choice's log-probability. It never decides a refund, and `not_a_dispute` is never automated: a fraud report read as a balance question is the costliest error.

Two senior agents labelled 2,400 messages independently; a team lead settled the 7% they disagreed on. Six thresholds, chosen on half and checked on the rest, target at most 2% in the wrong queue. Live since June, it routes 57% without a person; June–July audits found 1.7% wrong (95% interval 0.9–3.0%). About two and a half people's reading time moved to chargeback and fraud casework, agreed with the works council; nobody was cut.

**What the bank owns is six numbers, one rule, a version string and 2,400 labels its own people made.**

## A confidence number is a setting, not a fact

TypeSafe calls `confidence` "a statistic computed from the probability distribution". I ran jeff, an unofficial open-source reimplementation of Jev's API on a different open model, through TypeSafe's official SDK on 59 synthetic disputes.

A fixed 0.5 routes none at one setting and 37, seven wrong, at the other. The cut-offs that kept the routed set clean, 0.3 and 0.9, were picked in-sample.</figcaption>

At jeff's default server temperature of 3.2, confidence tops out at 0.4964; at temperature 1 the same answers reach 0.98. Two of the seven wrong answers above 0.5 are then fraud reports sent to `not_a_dispute`, at 0.74 and 0.78; a lost card went to `duplicate_charge` at 0.56. Another fraud report, at default settings:

```
jeff @ 34b32f9, default settings, via typesafe-sdk 0.7.1
state:  "Someone used my card details to buy clothes from an online
         store abroad. The card has never left my possession."
choice: not_a_dispute            confidence 0.1791
        not_a_dispute            0.2964
        fraud_card_not_present   0.2917
```

jeff lost clearly to two small local LLMs (33–42 of 59 against 53 and 55); this measures nothing about Jev. Its ranking stayed useful (AUROC 0.878 and 0.821), and a guaranteed error rate needs ranking plus same-distribution labels, not calibration (Geifman & El-Yaniv). Jev exposes no temperature; I infer its scale is set server-side too, so pin versions, as TypeSafe advises.

**A threshold belongs to a model version, the way it is served and your data — never to the documentation.**

## The replay

After Jev opened to everyone on 20 September, the replay ran on an ordinary self-serve account: a public signup, no preferential access, no contact beyond the form. The bank signed nothing.

Only 600 messages crossed, rewritten by hand with invented details; the DPO confirmed they held no personal data ([redaction alone](/writing/gdpr-presidio-llm-privacy) would not have done). We pinned `jev-1.13.0`, not the SDK's default `jev-latest`. Jev's cut-offs were chosen per language by cross-validation on the 600, the bank's on 1,200 other labels, an asymmetry that favours Jev.

| On 600 cleared messages (illustrative) | Jev | Bank's model |
|---|---|---|
| Right queue: all (German/English) | 76% (74/82) | 90% (90/91) |
| Acting alone at a copied 0.85 | 64%, 92% right | — |
| Acting alone at the bank's 2% bar | 28% (English 47, German 22) | 58% |

Public numbers run higher: Jev scores 79.7% on the Jevals Banking77 board and acts alone on 54% at 95.4% right, a looser bar than the bank's 98%. But TypeSafe says English is "where accuracy is currently best"; this set is three-quarters German. An English-language flow could decide differently.

That 0.85 comes from a voice-banking example in TypeSafe's routing pattern; here it erred at four times the bank's bar, consistent with an audit finding Jev overconfident on Banking77 (ECE 0.0936), though calibrated on CLINC150.

Because the policy existed, the replay took two days and one page: not now; rerun on the next pinned version.

## Where it breaks

**A threshold does not rescue a weaker model; it prices it honestly:** 28% against 58%.

**A pooled target hides a subgroup.** One Jev cut-off for both languages met the bar on average, as marginal guarantees do (Angelopoulos & Bates), while German alone ran over it (139 of 142 right). The bank's table now splits by language too.

**The asset depreciates anyway.** In August two app dropdown options became free text, and audited errors on the bank's own model rose to 3.7%, mostly `goods_not_received` confused with `not_as_described`; calibration is not guaranteed under shift (Ovadia et al.). Re-choosing the cut-offs took 600 fresh labels and sign-off.

**Small, clean numbers.** Jev's 163 of 166 right has a 95% Wilson interval of 94.8–99.4%, on text cleaner than real mail. The replay can reject for now; it cannot certify.

## Why this stays a replay

This bank judges dispute intake to support a critical or important function (DORA Art. 3(22)), which adds Arts 28(8), 29(2) and 30(3).

| The bank needs | TypeSafe's public terms, 23 Sep 2026 |
|---|---|
| Audit, supervisor included (Art.&nbsp;30(3)(e)(i)) | Personal-data controls only, yearly at most; none for the supervisor |
| Exit with a transition period (Arts 28(8), 30(3)(f)) | None: use ends at once on termination |
| Service levels (Art.&nbsp;30(3)(a)) | No SLA; "AS IS", "AS AVAILABLE" |
| Locations and data (Arts 29(2), 30(2)(b)–(c)) | No locations in the contract; US hosting and six US subprocessors; perpetual licence to derive "Telemetry", including "classifications" |
| Liability (not DORA) | Greater of 12 months' fees (about $1.40 here) or $50 |

**None of this says Jev is a bad model; it says a bank cannot sign the public contract for this flow as written.**

## What I would build

1. **Own the labels, and have people make them:** the MCA bars training a model to imitate Jev's output.
2. **Version the policy,** logging the `model` field and every probability; BaFin names unannounced model changes as a testing challenge.
3. **Make a version bump a recalibration ticket.**

```yaml
policy: card-dispute-routing
model: jev-1.13.0      # pinned; never an alias
labels: disputes-2400  # people-made, double-labelled, adjudicated
target: max 2% wrong queue, per language
never_auto: [not_a_dispute]
thresholds: per class × per language, chosen on the 'choose' half
checked_on: held-out half + monthly audit of 300
recalibrate_on: [new model version, upstream form change]
sign_off: model risk, as a parameter change
label_retention: per DPIA; rewritten set kept for replays
```

## Bottom line

The bank did not buy a model this year; it built a rule for when a model may act alone. Jev trailed, mostly in German, as TypeSafe's docs predict; its next version gets a replay. Use confidence gates — I do.

You rent the model. You own the threshold.
